# What makes the best lesson in the world?

**The interrogation that has to happen before we write the generation prompt.**

The current prompt tells the model to write a good lesson. It does not tell it what
*good* means in a way that can be checked, and it does not encode a single decision
about learning. That is why quality is a lottery: every pack is the model's own
theory of teaching, re-invented from scratch.

This document is the input to the rewrite. Part 1 is the question set — 130
questions that a lesson prompt implicitly answers whether we decide them or not.
Part 2 answers the ones research can settle, with sources. Part 3 is the ones only
the founder can settle, because they are product decisions and not empirical ones.

---

## Part 2 — What the evidence settles

### The sequencing question (Q27–36) has a real answer, and it is "both, in this order"

The literature splits into two camps that appear to contradict each other, and the
resolution is the single most useful thing in this document.

**Camp 1 — explicit instruction first.** Novices learn a procedure better when they
study worked examples than when they attempt unguided problem-solving; the guidance
lowers working-memory load enough for a schema to form. This is the worked-example
effect, and it is one of the most replicated findings in instructional psychology.

**Camp 2 — problem first.** Kapur's Productive Failure reverses the order: students
attempt a novel problem, generate incomplete or wrong solutions, and *then* receive
instruction. A systematic review of the controlled studies found problem-solving-first
produced significantly better **conceptual understanding and transfer** than
instruction-first, **without costing procedural fluency**.

They are not actually in conflict, because they optimise different outcomes:

| Goal | Better sequence |
|---|---|
| Procedural fluency, fast | Explanation → worked example → practice |
| Conceptual understanding + transfer | Attempt → fail → instruction → consolidation |

Kapur's own four-way analysis is the key: *unproductive success* is the trap where a
student is told exactly what to do, succeeds on the scaffolded task, and cannot do
anything different afterwards. That is precisely the failure mode of a worksheet-shaped
debate lesson.

**Two conditions decide whether the struggle is productive rather than demoralising:**
1. The student must have enough prior knowledge to generate *something* — a wrong
   method is required, a blank page is not.
2. The failure must be followed by a **consolidation phase** that explicitly contrasts
   what they tried with the canonical method. Productive failure without consolidation
   is just failure.

**So the answer to "evidence or explanation first" is:** put a short, well-designed
attempt *before* the explanation when the goal is understanding a concept (weighing,
warranting, what makes a rebuttal land) — and put the explanation first when the goal
is a procedure with a right answer (how to format a card, how to flow). And never run
the attempt without the consolidation that follows it.

### Guidance must fade, and staying high is actively harmful (Q18, 20, 90, 91)

The **expertise reversal effect**: support that helps a novice *hurts* a more
knowledgeable learner, because they must process the scaffold alongside knowledge they
already hold. This makes fading a requirement, not a nicety — a lesson 8 that scaffolds
like lesson 1 is worse than one that doesn't.

This applies directly to sentence frames (Q90). Research raises real cautions: one
classroom study found highly structured sentence starters steered attention *away* from
the content objective and did not produce collaborative academic discussion. Frames are
a scaffold with a shelf life, and must be differentiated by proficiency and withdrawn.

### The lesson skeleton that the evidence supports (Q13–26)

Rosenshine's principles are the closest thing to a consensus skeleton, drawn from
cognitive science *and* from observing the teachers whose students actually gained most:
daily review, small steps, many questions, models, guided practice, checks for
understanding, high success rate, scaffolds for hard tasks, independent practice, and
weekly/monthly review.

Two things in that list are routinely missing from AI-written packs and are worth
naming as requirements: **a high success rate during guided practice** (aim ~80%, not
"challenging"), and **review on a schedule** rather than only at the start.

### Practice must be distributed and retrieval-based (Q53–64)

The two techniques with the strongest evidence across 242 studies are **distributed
practice** and **practice testing** — well ahead of re-reading and highlighting, which
feel productive and are not. This has a direct structural consequence: a lesson that
practises only this week's skill is worse than one that interleaves earlier skills, and
the *series* has to carry a revisit schedule rather than leaving retention to chance.

The pack already does one thing right here — the review page checks *last* lesson's
skill, not this one's. That is spaced retrieval and it should be protected.

### Feedback has to answer three questions (Q97–108)

Hattie & Timperley: effective feedback answers **Where am I going? How am I going?
Where to next?** — and the third is the one most often missing. Feedback aimed at the
*self* ("good work") is the least effective level; task and process level are where the
gains are.

For a printed pack this is a structural requirement: every assessed thing needs a
visible success criterion (where am I going), a way to see current performance (how am
I going), and a stated next move (where to next). A rubric band with no next move is
half a feedback loop.

### Debate itself is a strong intervention — which raises the bar (Q65)

This matters for how ambitious the packs should be. A decade of Boston Public Schools
data found interscholastic policy debate improved ELA scores by 0.13 SD (~68% of a
year's 9th-grade learning), with gains concentrated in **analytical rather than rote**
subskills, largest for **previously low-achieving** students, and associated with
higher graduation and college enrolment.

Two implications. First, the mechanism is analytical, so packs that drill format and
terminology are leaving the actual benefit on the table. Second, the effect is largest
for the weakest students, so a pack that only works for the confident kid is failing
the population the activity most helps.

### Argumentation structure is teachable, whole-to-part (Q68)

Toulmin-based instruction improves argument quality when taught **holistic-to-specific**:
the whole model first, then the main elements, then sub-elements. That is an argument
for teaching the *shape of a complete argument* before drilling any one component —
and against a lesson 1 that spends forty minutes on "what is a claim".

## Part 3 — Decided (founder, 2026-08-08)

These are product decisions, not empirical ones. They are now settled and the
generation prompt encodes them.

### 1. Concept skills are taught attempt-first, with consolidation

Pages teaching a **concept** (weighing, warranting, what makes a rebuttal land) open
with a short attempt students are *expected to get partly wrong*, followed by
instruction that explicitly contrasts what they tried with the canonical method.
Pages teaching a **procedure** with a right answer (card format, flowing notation)
stay explanation-first.

Non-negotiable: an attempt page without its consolidation is a defect, not a style
choice. Productive failure minus consolidation is just failure. The teacher guide must
also *say* that the page is designed to be got wrong, or a coach will read a room of
struggling students as a broken lesson.

### 2. The floor matters more than the ceiling

Every production page must guarantee that the least confident student finishes
something: a steps checklist, frames, or a worked model alongside the prompt. This
follows the Boston finding that debate's gains are largest for previously low-achieving
students — a pack that only works for the confident kid is failing the students the
activity most helps. Fast-finisher extensions carry the strongest.

### 3. Evidence is given, and worked hard

Curated decks stay. 3–4 cards, each deployed on at least two later pages, returning to
the two strongest three or four times. This keeps citation integrity structurally
guaranteed (no student-fabricated sources) and keeps the lesson about *using* evidence
rather than hunting it. Finding sources is a later-series skill, not a lesson-1 one.

### 4. The generator may refuse an over-scoped skill

When a requested skill is too large for one lesson, the generator declines rather than
producing a shallow pack, and returns a proposed 2–3 lesson split. A shallow pack is
the expensive failure: it costs a build, teaches badly, and looks finished.

### Still open

- Lesson length: is 90 minutes a hard constraint or an average?
- How much teacher script is verbatim?
- Switch-sides: from which lesson?
- How much competitive-format specificity vs transferable argumentation?
